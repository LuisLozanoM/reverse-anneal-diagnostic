\begin{abstract}
Small subsystems are expected to relax when coupled to sufficiently large environments, but direct large-scale tests with independent control over bath size, coupling and disorder remain scarce.
Here we realise a controllable subsystem--environment experiment on two programmable quantum annealers by coupling a six-qubit subsystem to an on-chip environment during reverse annealing.
We find that the subsystem becomes initial-state independent within experimental resolution as the environment grows or the coupling increases, whereas quenched disorder and atypical environment preparation arrest this relaxation.
In the relaxed regime, the final subsystem readout is accurately described by a calibrated conditional Boltzmann marginal across graph geometries, coupling strengths, disorder levels and two QPU generations.
In a mixed-frustration regime, matched-temperature single-spin-flip Glauber dynamics fails to reproduce the observed relaxation rate, consistent with a transverse-field-assisted relaxation pathway.
These results separate the dynamics of relaxation from the form of the relaxed readout and establish programmable quantum annealers as controllable platforms for studying subsystem relaxation, effective thermal readout, and memory arrest.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

How thermal equilibrium emerges from microscopic quantum dynamics is a central question in statistical mechanics~\cite{deutsch1991quantum,srednicki1994chaos}.
The eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) offers a theoretical framework: for generic, non-integrable Hamiltonians, small subsystems relax toward thermal distributions when coupled to a sufficiently large environment, even in a globally isolated system~\cite{rigol2008thermalization}.
Many-body localization (MBL) can arrest this relaxation when quenched disorder exceeds a critical threshold~\cite{nandkishore2015mbl,abanin2019mbl}.
Experimental signatures of ETH have been observed in cold-atom systems~\cite{kaufman2016quantum}, and recent Rydberg-array experiments have probed bath-size-dependent signatures of Hilbert-space ergodicity~\cite{shaw2025ergodicity}.
MBL has been probed in optical lattices~\cite{schreiber2015mbl} and trapped-ion simulators~\cite{smith2016mbl}, but simultaneously tuning bath size, coupling strength, and disorder at large scale remains challenging on these platforms.
The theoretical foundations connecting subsystem relaxation to the structure of the global quantum state are well established~\cite{popescu2006entanglement,goldstein2006canonical,dalessio2016quantum}, yet direct large-scale experimental tests remain scarce.

Programmable quantum annealers, originally designed for optimization, have recently emerged as versatile quantum simulators.
D-Wave devices have demonstrated coherent quantum annealing in two-thousand-qubit Ising chains~\cite{king2022coherent}, quantum critical dynamics in five-thousand-qubit spin glasses~\cite{king2023critical}, false-vacuum bubble nucleation~\cite{vodeb2025false_vacuum}, and finite-temperature critical exponents via quantum annealing~\cite{sathe2026criticality}.
Their reverse-annealing mode enables controlled preparation and dynamical probing of many-body quantum states~\cite{marshall2019pausing,chen2020pausing,mehta2025unraveling}.
A recent review explicitly noted that whether a sufficiently large annealer can act as an effective thermal bath for its own subsystems remains an open question, and that no such research had been performed on annealing hardware~\cite{kendon2026qacm}.
Temperature-estimation, freeze-out and Gibbs-sampling studies have shown that annealer outputs can sometimes resemble effective Boltzmann distributions, while also exposing local/global and scale-dependent limitations~\cite{raymond2016global,marshall2017freezeout,marshall2019pausing,nelson2022gibbs,vuffray2022boltzmann,grattan2025thermometry}.
Recent work on finite-temperature criticality through quantum annealing~\cite{teza2025finite}, reverse-anneal memory erasure~\cite{pelofske2025erasing} and programmable localization~\cite{dacafilho2022localization} provides closely related but distinct context.

Here we address this open question experimentally on two D-Wave QPUs with different topologies and energy scales.
We partition the annealer into a small subsystem~$S$ ($|S|=6$) coupled to a programmable environment~$E$ (up to $|E|=50$), prepare distinct initial states of both~$S$ and~$E$, and use reverse annealing to probe the dynamics at a controlled pause point.
We ask two questions.
First, under what conditions does the subsystem become initial-state independent?
Second, when relaxation occurs, what form does the converged subsystem readout take?
We find a tunable crossover from memory retention to relaxation with increasing bath size and coupling, arrest of this crossover by disorder and atypical bath preparation, and a calibrated conditional thermal law for the relaxed readout.
Mixed frustration then reveals that the route to relaxation can be quantitatively distinct from matched-temperature single-spin-flip classical dynamics.

We emphasize that D-Wave QPUs operate as open quantum systems: the cryogenic environment, control noise, and the anneal protocol itself contribute to the dynamics~\cite{albash2018adiabatic}.
Our results therefore demonstrate subsystem relaxation during reverse annealing---a process involving both transverse-field dynamics and environmental coupling---rather than thermalization in a strictly isolated system.

Our experiment uses D-Wave's reverse-annealing protocol (Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}b).
The QPU implements the time-dependent Hamiltonian $H(s) = -\frac{A(s)}{2}\sum_i \sigma_x^i + \frac{B(s)}{2}H_P$, where $H_P = \sum_i h_i \sigma_z^i + \sum_{ij} J_{ij}\sigma_z^i\sigma_z^j$ is the programmable problem Hamiltonian and $s\in[0,1]$ parameterizes the anneal~\cite{dwave2024reverse_anneal}.
In reverse annealing, the system is initialized in a classical state at $s=1$, ramped to a pause point~$s_p < 1$ where quantum fluctuations are reintroduced, held for a pause time~$t_p$, and returned to $s=1$ for readout.

We partition the qubits into a subsystem~$S$ ($|S|=6$) and an environment~$E$ ($|E|$ up to 50), connected through boundary couplers of strength~$\lambda$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}c).
Internal couplings are ferromagnetic ($J=-1$), and quenched disorder is introduced as random longitudinal fields $h_i\sim\mathcal{U}[-W,W]$.
Different initial preparations of~$S$ (all spins up, all down, or random) are used while holding~$E$ fixed.
The key observable is the memory order parameter~$\mathcal{M}$, defined as the maximum pairwise total variation distance (TVD) between the marginal distributions $P_S$ obtained from different initial states (see Methods).
$\mathcal{M}\to 0$ signals initial-state independence; $\mathcal{M}= 1$ indicates complete memory retention.

\subsection*{Subsystem relaxation with tunable environment}

With the protocol and subsystem partition defined, we first show that enlarging and more strongly coupling the environment drives the subsystem toward initial-state independence within experimental resolution on both QPU generations (Fig.~\ref{fig:subsystem}).
On the Advantage2 QPU ($s_p=0.4$, $t_p=100\,\mu$s), the memory order parameter drops sharply with environment size: from $\mathcal{M}=1.0$ at $|E|=4$ to $\mathcal{M}=0.005$ at $|E|=8$ and $\mathcal{M}<0.005$ for $|E|\geq 16$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:subsystem}a, blue circles).
The Advantage\_system6.4 QPU shows the same qualitative trend, with a slightly higher residual memory at intermediate~$|E|$ consistent with its lower energy-to-temperature ratio (Fig.~\ref{fig:subsystem}a, red squares).

The coupling strength~$\lambda$ provides an independent control parameter (Fig.~\ref{fig:subsystem}b).
On Advantage2, memory decreases monotonically from $\mathcal{M}=0.94$ at $\lambda=0.05$ through $0.53$ at $\lambda=0.2$ to $\mathcal{M}<0.001$ for $\lambda\geq 0.3$.
On System~6.4, a similar crossover occurs at lower~$\lambda$, consistent with the weaker effective coupling at this QPU's energy scale.

To probe the role of the pause-point Hamiltonian, we sweep~$s_p$ on both QPUs (Fig.~\ref{fig:subsystem}c,d).
The crossover curves display the same sigmoidal shape on both platforms: as $s_p$ increases from 0.35 to 0.50, the crossover coupling~$\lambda_c$ shifts to larger values, reflecting the decreasing transverse field.
In~situ measurement of the dimensionless energy scale $\beta_\mathrm{eff}=B(s_p)R/2k_BT$ at $s_p=0.4$ yields $\beta_\mathrm{eff}=6.93\pm 0.20$ (Advantage2) and $4.46\pm 0.26$ (System~6.4) (Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder}c; see Methods).
The ratio of~1.55 is consistent with the different hardware energy scales.
The systematic shift of crossover thresholds between QPUs, together with their common functional form across~$s_p$, suggests that the relaxation behaviour is governed by the Hamiltonian parameters at the pause point rather than by platform-specific details.  We emphasise, however, that rescaling Advantage2 couplings by the measured ratio $\beta_\mathrm{eff}^{\mathrm{S6.4}}/\beta_\mathrm{eff}^{\mathrm{A2}}=0.59$ does not collapse the cross-QPU crossover curves onto a single master curve, and a residual $\sim 3\times$ gap between the two platforms persists at $s_p=0.4$ (Supplementary Information); a full quantitative collapse would require matching both~$\beta_\mathrm{eff}$ and the transverse-field ratio~$A(s_p)/B(s_p)$, which differ between QPUs at the same~$s_p$.  The present evidence therefore supports qualitative cross-QPU agreement with thresholds shifted by the measured energy-scale ratio, rather than strict universality in a scaling-collapse sense.

\subsection*{Environment-state dependence and kinetics}

Having established that relaxation can be tuned by environment size, coupling, and pause point, we next ask which environment states actually produce this initial-state independence and on what timescale it develops.
To test whether the environment acts as a preparation-independent bath, we repeat the experiment at the relaxed point ($|E|=50$, $\lambda=0.5$) with four different initial preparations of~$E$: all-up, all-down, random, and domain wall, while varying the initial state of~$S$ as before.
When~$E$ is initialized in an ordered state (all-up or all-down), $S$~becomes initial-state independent within experimental resolution ($\mathcal{M}=0.000$).
However, when~$E$ is initialized in a disordered configuration (random or domain wall), residual memory persists ($\mathcal{M}=0.39$ and $0.65$, respectively).
Furthermore, $P_S$~depends on which~$E$ preparation was used (cross-preparation TVD$\,= 1.0$).
This indicates that the on-chip environment is a finite-size partner, not a universal thermal bath: the initial microstate of~$E$ influences the subsystem's fate.

Pause-time kinetics confirm that this distinction is not a timescale effect.
When~$E$ is ordered, subsystem relaxation is rapid: $\mathcal{M}$ drops from $0.017$ at $t_p=1\,\mu$s to $<0.001$ by $t_p=5\,\mu$s and remains zero out to $t_p=500\,\mu$s.
When~$E$ is in a random configuration, memory fluctuates between $0.1$ and $0.7$ across the same range of~$t_p$ with no systematic decay, indicating that relaxation does not occur on any timescale accessible in this experiment.

We also test environment states produced by the QPU's own forward-annealing dynamics.
Forward-annealing the $E$-only Hamiltonian (with~$S$ decoupled) yields low-energy, ordered samples (magnetization $\pm 1$, ground-state energy); using these as the environment initial state for reverse annealing produced $\mathcal{M}=0.000$ on each of the tested forward-annealed environment preparations.
This shows that the QPU's natural sampling tendency produces environment states that drive subsystem relaxation, and that the relaxation--arrest distinction is controlled by the environment's order, not by the method of preparation.

These observations are consistent with the expectation that subsystem relaxation depends on the environment's energy sector~\cite{linden2009equilibration,popescu2006entanglement}: for the ferromagnetic Hamiltonian, ordered product states lie near the ground-state manifold and can serve as effective baths, while random product states occupy a high-energy sector with qualitatively different dynamics.

\subsection*{Disorder arrests relaxation}

This pronounced sensitivity to the environment's initial energy sector suggests that disorder should compete directly with the same relaxation mechanism, which we test next by adding random longitudinal fields (Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder}a).
At fixed $|E|=50$ and $\lambda=0.5$, the memory order parameter remains near zero for $W\leq 1.0$ on both QPUs, then rises: to $\mathcal{M}=0.74$ at $W=2.0$ on Advantage2 and $\mathcal{M}=0.50$ at $W=1.5$ on System~6.4.
The lower threshold on System~6.4 is consistent with its smaller~$\beta_\mathrm{eff}$.
To assess the robustness of the disorder-arrest effect, we repeat the Advantage2 $W$-scan with five independent random-field realizations.
The disorder-averaged memory is $\mathcal{M}=0.000\pm 0.000$ for $W\leq 1.0$, $0.001\pm 0.001$ at $W=1.5$, and $0.125\pm 0.103$ at $W=2.0$ (mean $\pm$ s.d.\ across realizations).
The large variance at $W=2.0$ reflects the expected sample-to-sample fluctuations near a crossover, and supports the conclusion that the disorder-arrest effect is robust in the mean but realization-dependent in magnitude.

The full crossover diagram in the $(\lambda, W)$ plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:disorder}b), constructed from 72 grid points on Advantage2, reveals a boundary separating initial-state-independent (memory$\,\approx 0$) and memory-retaining (memory$\,> 0.1$) regions.
Stronger coupling generally pushes the disorder threshold higher, consistent with the intuition that a more strongly coupled environment is harder to localize.
We note that the disorder-arrest threshold is graph-dependent: native Zephyr subgraphs, whose boundary connectivity between~$S$ and~$E$ is roughly an order of magnitude denser than the random 3-regular logical graphs studied here, relax across a broader range of~$W$ (Supplementary Information).
The lower connectivity of the random 3-regular logical graph therefore provides a more stringent test of disorder arrest, and the native-graph results constrain embedding-induced systematics without contradicting the main conclusion.
This crossover between relaxing and memory-retaining regimes is reminiscent of the delocalized--localized boundary predicted by MBL theory, though we caution that the open-system nature of the QPU and the finite pause time preclude direct identification with an MBL transition.

\subsection*{Classical and small-system controls}

To place the relaxation crossovers and the thermal-marginal endpoint on firmer footing, we compare the data with classical and small-system controls that test whether the observed initial-state independence can arise without quantum mixing (Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines}).

As a purely classical reference, we simulated single-spin-flip Glauber dynamics at the device effective temperature across the ranges of system size, coupling strength, and disorder studied experimentally, and found $\mathcal{M}=1.0$ in every case (Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines}a), indicating complete retention of the subsystem's initial-state memory.
The same conclusion holds for continuous-spin $O(3)$ spin-vector Monte Carlo~\cite{shin2014svmc}, which is often invoked to argue that semiclassical models can reproduce D-Wave dynamics: at the device effective temperature, SVMC likewise shows $\mathcal{M}=1.0$ across our parameter range (Supplementary Information Sec.~3.4).

Exact diagonalization (ED) of the closed quantum Hamiltonian for small systems ($N=8$--$12$, $|S|=4$) shows the same qualitative trend as the QPU data.
At $s_p=0.4$, $\mathcal{M}$ decreases with system size: for $t_p=0.5\,\mu$s, $\mathcal{M}$ falls from $0.47$ at $N=8$ to $0.13$ at $N=12$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines}b).
By contrast, at $s_p=0.5$, $\mathcal{M}$ remains above $0.94$ for all sizes, consistent with much weaker mixing.
A complementary Lindblad master-equation treatment with local thermal jump operators at the device temperature (Supplementary Information) reproduces the same qualitative trends across the tractable system sizes.
This agreement does not establish that the QPU dynamics are themselves unitary, but it supports quantum fluctuations as the primary ingredient enabling subsystem relaxation~\cite{mehta2025dwave}.

A further signature of quantum control appears in the pause-point dependence of the coupling crossover.
On both QPUs, the threshold coupling $\lambda_c$ required to erase the subsystem's initial-state memory shifts systematically with $s_p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines}c), such that deeper pauses, with larger transverse-field weight, produce relaxation at weaker coupling.
A mechanism governed purely by the classical problem Hamiltonian $H_P$ would not generate this $s_p$ dependence, although open-system effects, including $s_p$-dependent decoherence, may also contribute to the observed crossover~\cite{mehta2025dwave}.

\subsection*{Barrier crossing under mixed frustration}

The results above establish both that the subsystem relaxes and what it relaxes to.
We now ask a sharper dynamical question: can the QPU relax through frustrated landscapes that are not traversed efficiently by matched-temperature single-spin-flip classical dynamics?

To probe this, we introduce mixed frustration: the subsystem~$S$ couplers are sign-flipped with probability~$p_S$ (Edwards--Anderson bond disorder), while the environment~$E$ remains ferromagnetic.
This creates a multi-modal classical landscape within~$S$ while preserving the bath character of~$E$.
At $p_S=0.5$ and $W=1.0$, the QPU relaxes $14/20$ instances ($70\%$; $|S|=4$, $|E|=8$, $N=12$, $\lambda=0.5$), while single-spin-flip Glauber dynamics at the calibrated device temperature ($\beta_\mathrm{eff}=7.2$) relaxes only $2/20$ ($10\%$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:barrier}a).
A fine Glauber temperature sweep reveals that classical dynamics requires $\beta\approx 1.5$ ($T\approx 0.67$), roughly $4.8\times$ the device temperature, to approximate the QPU's relaxation rate.
Cross-QPU replication on Advantage\_system6.4 ($\beta_\mathrm{eff}=4.29$) gives the same qualitative separation, with the second QPU relaxing $10/20$ instances ($50\%$) versus $5/20$ ($25\%$) for matched-temperature Glauber, and Glauber reaching $50\%$ at $\beta\approx 2.5$ ($T\approx 0.4$), an effective-temperature gap of $1.7\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:barrier}b).

Exact enumeration of all $2^{12}=4096$ configurations clarifies the relevant classical barrier scale: the frustrated instances have on average $9.2$ local minima separated by single-spin-flip barriers of mean height $\Delta=1.93$, giving $\beta_\mathrm{eff}\Delta\approx 14$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:barrier}c).
Classical thermal activation over these barriers is exponentially suppressed at the device temperature; the QPU's transverse field at the pause point is consistent with an alternative relaxation pathway.
The mixed-frustration design further shows that relaxation persists across tested frustration densities---from $p_S=0$ (ferromagnetic) through $p_S=0.5$ (full subsystem EA)---with QPU relaxation rates spanning $53$--$92\%$ in the Supplementary mixed-frustration sweep; the matched-temperature Glauber comparison is shown for the $p_S=0.5$, $W=1.0$ working point (Supplementary Information).
By contrast, full Edwards--Anderson disorder across the entire $S$+$E$ system produced zero relaxed instances among the $826$ completed conditions across two QPUs, indicating that a fully frustrated environment did not act as a bath under the tested reverse-anneal protocol.

\section*{Discussion}

We have shown that a programmable quantum annealer can drive subsystem relaxation during reverse annealing under controlled bath conditions, and that this relaxation is arrested by disorder and atypical environment preparation.
The central result is therefore not only that the subsystem becomes initial-state independent, but that the relaxed readout follows a calibrated conditional thermal law across graph geometries, coupling strengths, disorder levels and two QPU generations.
This separates the question of \emph{whether} relaxation occurs from the question of \emph{what} the relaxed endpoint looks like.

\paragraph{Calibrated thermal readout.}
Across $97\%$ of relaxed ($\mathcal{M}\le 0.05$) instances in the ferromagnetic Phase 6 main sweep, the measured subsystem marginal agrees with the classical conditional Boltzmann distribution $P_S^\mathrm{th}(\sigma_S\mid\sigma_E{=}\mathbf{1})$ at the calibrated $\beta_\mathrm{eff}$ with $D_\mathrm{TV}<0.05$, and a tighter near-sampling-floor subset of relaxed disorder seeds reaches $D_\mathrm{TV}\sim 10^{-3}$ (see Effective thermal marginal subsection).
We describe this as a consistency check of the conditional thermal-marginal hypothesis on the relaxed subset rather than as an unconditional proof of thermalization: non-relaxed conditions have much larger $D_\mathrm{TV}$ against the same target (median $0.35$ across 381 non-relaxed conditions), and three relaxed conditions fail the thermal-marginal test with $D_\mathrm{TV}>0.05$---identifying a distinct dynamical-trapping failure mode that the memory order parameter alone would not detect.
Only $113$ of the $494$ Phase~6 main-sweep conditions satisfy the relaxation criterion $\mathcal{M}\le 0.05$, reflecting the broad parameter ranges swept (system size, coupling strength, disorder strength, Hamiltonian energy scale) relative to the finite-time, finite-size nature of the experiment; the thermal-marginal law is claimed only on this relaxed subset.
This result holds across graph geometry, subsystem size, coupling strength, Hamiltonian energy scale, and QPU generation.
A small frustrated pilot hints at a different pattern: in its two relaxed Edwards--Anderson instances, the conditional quantum reduced Gibbs target outperforms the classical conditional target by roughly an order of magnitude.  The pilot is underpowered (only two relaxed instances) and motivates a larger frustrated campaign to establish the effect; the ferromagnetic data, by contrast, do not distinguish the two predictions at the sampling floor.

\paragraph{Barrier crossing under mixed frustration.}
A further result concerns the relaxation mechanism under mixed frustration, where the QPU differs quantitatively from matched-temperature Glauber dynamics.
When~$S$ carries Edwards--Anderson bond disorder ($p_S=0.5$) while~$E$ remains ferromagnetic, the QPU relaxes $\sim\!70\%$ of instances at the calibrated device temperature ($\beta_\mathrm{eff}=7.2$), versus $\sim\!10\%$ for single-spin-flip Glauber at the same temperature.
A fine temperature sweep shows that Glauber requires roughly $4.8\times$ the device temperature to match the QPU relaxation rate on Advantage2, and $1.7\times$ on System~6.4.
Exact enumeration of the $N=12$ landscape gives on average $9.2$ local minima with mean single-spin-flip barrier $\Delta=1.93$, so $\beta_\mathrm{eff}\Delta\approx 14$---well into the Arrhenius-suppressed regime.
The QPU's transverse field at the pause point is consistent with an alternative relaxation pathway, and cross-QPU replication supports the interpretation that the effect is not specific to a single processor~\cite{denchev2016computational,boixo2016tunneling}.
These ratios are empirical comparisons for the SDK-default submission path used in the QPU runs; a paired re-verification of the working point with \texttt{auto\_scale=False} reproduces the same rates within seed variability on both QPUs (Supplementary Information), so the separation between QPU relaxation and matched-temperature local Glauber dynamics is preserved under the regenerated submission path.
Schedule engineering, scaling, and native frustrated motifs remain beyond the scope of the present study.

\paragraph{Open-system caveats and outlook.}
D-Wave QPUs operate as open quantum systems: the cryogenic bath, flux noise, and the time-dependent anneal schedule all participate in the dynamics~\cite{albash2018adiabatic,mehta2025dwave,mehta2025unraveling,aronoff2026coherence}.
Our results demonstrate subsystem relaxation in the specific context of reverse-anneal dynamics, which involves both transverse-field evolution and environmental decoherence.
The dynamical separation from Glauber is specific to single-spin-flip classical dynamics at matched temperature; a referee may reasonably ask whether more sophisticated classical algorithms (parallel tempering, cluster moves, simulated quantum annealing) could close the gap.
We note, however, that single-spin-flip Glauber is a standard local classical reference for D-Wave benchmarking~\cite{shin2014svmc,albash2018adiabatic}, and the effective-temperature gap is quantitative, with ratios of $4.8\times$ on Advantage2 and $1.7\times$ on Advantage\_system6.4.

A native-graph replication on the actual QPU topology (no minor embedding) supports the thermal-marginal law: across $5$ relaxed native Zephyr conditions ($N=12$, $|S|=4$, $\lambda=0.5$), the mean classical conditional $D_\mathrm{TV}=0.0015$, indicating that the thermal readout is not solely an embedding artefact.
Several directions follow naturally: a bath-ensemble experiment with multiple certified low-energy environment microstates would strengthen the ``effective bath'' interpretation beyond ordered boundary conditions, and extending the frustrated campaign to larger $N$ and native topologies would test whether the barrier-crossing effect scales.
We further note that the environment size required for relaxation is geometry-dependent: native Zephyr subgraphs, with roughly an order of magnitude denser boundary connectivity, relax at smaller $|E|$ on the same logical problem than the random 3-regular graphs reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:subsystem}a, and conversely at $|E|=20$, $\lambda=0.5$ a direct native-qubit submission remains memory-retaining while the embedded random-3-regular submission at larger $|E|=50$ relaxes (Supplementary Information).
The relaxation threshold in $|E|$ therefore characterises the effective boundary coupling density of the chosen graph family, not a universal qubit-count threshold.

Our results address an open question posed in recent reviews~\cite{kendon2026qacm}: whether a sufficiently large quantum annealer can act as an effective bath for its own subsystems.
We find that it can, under ordered low-energy environment preparations, and that this bath-like behaviour is arrested by disorder and atypical environment states.
When relaxation occurs, the relaxed readout follows a calibrated classical thermal marginal across graph geometries, coupling strengths, disorder levels and two QPU generations.
In a mixed-frustration regime, the route to that endpoint is quantitatively distinct from matched-temperature single-spin-flip classical dynamics, consistent with transverse-field-assisted barrier crossing.
These findings establish programmable quantum annealers as experimental platforms for studying subsystem relaxation, connecting quantum annealing to statistical mechanics and open-system quantum dynamics.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Quantum processing units}

Experiments were performed on two D-Wave QPUs~\cite{dwave2024system_docs}.  The Zephyr-topology
processor was operated as \texttt{Advantage2\_system1.13} (4{,}579 active
qubits, $\beta_\mathrm{eff}=6.93\pm 0.20$ at $s_p=0.4$) for the main
environment-size, coupling, disorder and pause-point sweeps of
Figs.~\ref{fig:subsystem}--\ref{fig:disorder} and~\ref{fig:baselines}.  On 2026-04-10 D-Wave flagged qubit 4374 as operating out of
spec between 2026-04-08 21:30 PT and 2026-04-10 10:50 PT and replaced the
solver endpoint with \texttt{Advantage2\_system1}, in which qubit 4374
and its couplers are removed from the working graph.  The Phase 6
Gibbs-fit campaign of Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal_marginal} was submitted on this renamed solver after
the incident window, with qubit 4374 therefore excluded from every
embedding; an in-situ recalibration under the procedure described below
gives $\beta_\mathrm{eff}=7.219\pm 0.063$ for that campaign, and the
frustrated pilot reported in the Effective thermal marginal subsection
uses an independently recalibrated
$\beta_\mathrm{eff}=7.331\pm0.198$.  The Pegasus-topology processor
\texttt{Advantage\_system6.4} (5{,}612 active qubits) has
$\beta_\mathrm{eff}=4.46\pm 0.26$ at $s_p=0.4$ for the main paper and
$\beta_\mathrm{eff}=4.289\pm 0.294$ for the Phase 6 cross-QPU
replication.
The dimensionless energy scale $\beta_\mathrm{eff}=B(s_p)R/2k_BT$ was
measured in~situ by submitting single-qubit probe problems with a known
bias $h=0.5$ under reverse annealing at $s_p=0.4$ with
\texttt{auto\_scale=False}, and extracting
$\beta_\mathrm{eff} = \ln(n_\downarrow/n_\uparrow)/(2h)$ from
5{,}000 reads for each of two initial states, averaged over three probe
qubits drawn from the solver nodelist.
Here $B(s_p)$ is the problem energy scale at the pause point, $R$ is the
Hamiltonian rescaling factor from \texttt{auto\_scale}, $k_B$ is the
Boltzmann constant, and $T$ is the physical device temperature.  We
emphasise that $\beta_\mathrm{eff}$ is a protocol-level readout parameter
rather than a refrigerator temperature; the separate main-paper and
Phase~6 values reflect independent calibration sessions, not a physical
temperature drift.  The per-QPU anneal-schedule coefficients
$A(s_p)$, $B(s_p)$ used in the Phase 6 quantum reduced Gibbs diagnostic
were extracted directly from the solver schedule spreadsheets published
by D-Wave~\cite{dwave2024system_docs}, yielding $A(s_p)/B(s_p)=0.260$ for Advantage2 and $0.259$
for Advantage\_system6.4 at $s_p=0.4$.

\subsection*{Hamiltonian family and embedding}

The problem Hamiltonian is a random 3-regular Ising model: $H_P = \sum_i h_i \sigma_z^i + \sum_{\langle ij \rangle} J_{ij}\sigma_z^i\sigma_z^j$, with internal couplings $J_{ij}=-1$ (ferromagnetic), boundary couplings between $S$ and $E$ scaled by $\lambda\in[0,1]$, and quenched disorder $h_i\sim\mathcal{U}[-W,W]$.
The main environment-size, coupling, disorder and pause-depth sweeps (Figs.~\ref{fig:subsystem}--\ref{fig:baselines}) use $|S|=6$ with $|E|$ up to $50$; the Phase 6 thermal-marginal and Phase 7 frustrated-landscape campaigns use $|S|=4$ small-system instances at $N\le 12$ to permit exact classical and quantum reference computations.  In all cases $S$ is a connected subgraph and $E$ comprises the remaining qubits.
Logical graphs were embedded onto each QPU using \texttt{EmbeddingComposite}~\cite{cai2014minor} with the default chain strength from \texttt{uniform\_torque\_compensation}; chain-break dynamics and embedding-induced sampling bias are discussed in~\cite{marshall2020embedding}.
We note that embedding introduces chains of physical qubits for each logical variable, and chain breaks can affect the effective coupling landscape.
The default chain strength is determined by the logical problem's connectivity and bias magnitudes, and may differ between Zephyr and Pegasus embeddings even for the same logical graph.

\subsection*{Reverse-anneal protocol}

All experiments use a four-point reverse-anneal schedule~\cite{dwave2024reverse_anneal}: $(0, 1.0) \to (5\,\mu\mathrm{s}, s_p) \to (5+t_p\,\mu\mathrm{s}, s_p) \to (10+t_p\,\mu\mathrm{s}, 1.0)$, with \texttt{reinitialize\_state=True}.
Default parameters: $s_p=0.4$, $t_p=100\,\mu$s, 1{,}000--2{,}000 reads per configuration.
Unless otherwise stated, initial states of $S$ were varied (all-up, all-down, random) while $E$ was initialized all-up; the environment-preparation controls (all-down, random, domain wall, forward-annealed) and the bath-ensemble and frustrated sub-experiments use the preparations described in the Results and Supplementary Information.
The submission path differs across sub-experiments and is important for the interpretation of absolute threshold values.
The Phase~6 $\beta_\mathrm{eff}$ calibration probes were submitted with \texttt{auto\_scale=False} so that the single-qubit bias $h=0.5$ is programmed exactly.
The Phase~6 thermal-marginal native-Zephyr conditions use direct physical-qubit submissions without chain couplers, so that the programmed and nominal $(h,J)$ coincide.
Legacy Phase~3 and Phase~4 sweeps (Figs.~\ref{fig:subsystem}--\ref{fig:baselines}) and the Phase~7 mixed-frustration working point (Fig.~\ref{fig:barrier}) were submitted through the SDK-default path, in which the minor-embedding composite and the automatic Hamiltonian rescaling (\texttt{auto\_scale=True}) are both active; the rescaling applies a uniform linear factor to all logical and chain couplers whenever any coupler would otherwise exceed the solver's $j$-range~\cite{dwave2024system_docs}.
We therefore performed paired \texttt{auto\_scale}-on/off spot-checks on \texttt{Advantage2\_system1} to characterise the impact of this rescaling on the memory order parameter: (i) six Phase~4 working points on direct physical-qubit submissions (no chains); (ii) five additional transition-region working points $\lambda\in\{0.15,0.20,0.25,0.30,0.50\}$ at $|E|=20$ on direct physical-qubit submissions; (iii) four back-to-back repeats of an embedded 26-qubit random-3-regular problem with explicit \texttt{chain\_strength}$=1$ at $\lambda=0.2$.
Outside the transition region ($\mathcal{M}\approx 0$ or $\mathcal{M}\approx 1$), the two \texttt{auto\_scale} settings give $|\Delta\mathcal{M}|\lesssim 0.04$; inside the transition region the rescaling shifts the effective crossover in~$\lambda$, with $\Delta\mathcal{M}$ reaching $\approx 0.98$ on the embedded problem at $\lambda=0.2$ in a reproducible manner (Supplementary Information).
The qualitative conclusions of the paper---the monotonic decrease of $\mathcal{M}$ with $|E|$ and $\lambda$, the arrest by disorder and by atypical environment preparations, the $s_p$-dependence of the crossover, the qualitative cross-QPU agreement, and the thermal-marginal agreement on relaxed conditions where the sample distribution is already at the stable end state---are preserved under both settings.
The quantitative placement of the crossover thresholds~$\lambda_c$ reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:subsystem} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines}, and the specific temperature-gap ratios reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:barrier}, inherit a rescaling-dependent systematic characterised by the spot-check; we report these values as empirical quantities for the SDK-default submission path used in the legacy campaigns, not as universal, rescaling-independent numbers.
As a separate test of intrinsic control-error (ICE) bias we ran the same four transition-region conditions at four independent random spin-reversal gauges with \texttt{auto\_scale=False}; the gauge-averaged memory order parameter has standard deviation $\le 0.02$ across gauges at every tested condition (Supplementary Information), indicating that ICE bias is a subdominant source of uncertainty in the main-text observables.
In the public code release the reverse-anneal driver defaults to \texttt{auto\_scale=False} so that future submissions retain an exact nominal-to-programmed correspondence.
For the barrier-crossing working point of Fig.~\ref{fig:barrier} we further re-ran the full 10-seed campaign with \texttt{auto\_scale=False} on both QPUs (Supplementary Information); the regenerated relaxation rates ($8/10$ on Advantage2 and $7/10$ on Advantage\_system6.4) are consistent with or exceed the legacy rates ($14/20$ and $10/20$), so the $4.8\times$ and $1.7\times$ temperature gaps versus matched-temperature Glauber are preserved under the regenerated submission path.

\subsection*{Observables and statistical analysis}

The marginal distribution $P_S(\sigma_S) = \sum_{\sigma_E} P(\sigma_S, \sigma_E)$ is computed from QPU samples by projecting onto the subsystem qubit indices, using the variable ordering returned by the sampler.
The memory order parameter is $\mathcal{M} = \max_{a,b}\,\mathrm{TVD}(P_S^{(a)}, P_S^{(b)})$, where $\mathrm{TVD}(P,Q) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_x |P(x)-Q(x)|$.
Uncertainty estimates on $\mathcal{M}$ are obtained by bootstrap resampling (200 resamples with replacement from the QPU reads), and are used for the error bars shown in the main-text figures; per-condition bootstrap standard deviations and disorder-averaged dispersions are tabulated in the Supplementary Information.

\subsection*{Classical baselines}

\emph{Glauber dynamics:} single-spin-flip Monte Carlo with acceptance probability $p = [1+\exp(\Delta E/k_BT)]^{-1}$ at the device effective temperature; 10{,}000 sweeps, 2{,}000 samples after equilibration.
\emph{Exact diagonalization:} the transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian $H(s_p)$ was constructed as a sparse matrix for $N\leq 12$ and time-evolved via \texttt{expm\_multiply} (SciPy).  The schedule coefficients $A(s_p)$ and $B(s_p)$ in GHz are absorbed into $H(s_p)$ directly, and the physical pause time $t_p$ (in $\mu$s) is converted to the dimensionless time argument of $\exp(-iHt)$ via $t_\mathrm{nat}=2\pi\times 10^{3}\,t_{\mu s}$, where the factor of $2\pi\times 10^3$ reconciles GHz with inverse microseconds (in units where $\hbar=1$).
Subsystem marginals were obtained by tracing out environment degrees of freedom.
\emph{Spin-vector Monte Carlo:} continuous O(3) Heisenberg spins with Metropolis updates on the unit sphere, using $H = \sum_i h_i n_i^z + \sum_{ij} J_{ij}(\mathbf{n}_i\cdot\mathbf{n}_j)$ and $z$-axis projected measurement.
Full details of all baselines are in the Supplementary Information.
